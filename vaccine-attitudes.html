Vaccine Attitudes Survey

An 800-person survey of vaccine conspiracy endorsement compared how two audience segments rated the same counter-evidence coming from five different kinds of sources.

Sample
800 U.S. adults
Prolific; quota-balanced between vaccine-hesitant and vaccine-supportive adults
Design
Within-subjects survey (2 claims × 5 evidence sources)
Analysis
R (tidyverse, lme4); linear mixed models; Tableau Public
Headline finding

Conspiracy endorsers and non-endorsers trusted informal sources about equally. Non-endorsers trusted institutional sources (like medical journals) about 2.4 times as much as endorsers did.

This study asked whether people who reject a claim are rejecting the evidence itself or only certain sources of it.

02Methodology & scope

Rating counter-evidence from five sources

Each participant did this twice, once for a claim that the government is lying about the COVID-19 vaccine’s safety and effectiveness and once for the same claim about pharmaceutical companies. They rated their belief in the claim, then saw evidence against it from five sources. After each source, they rated how much they trusted it and how much they now believed the claim.

03Insights & recommendations

What the data show, and what to do about it

1 Source trust

Endorsers distrusted institutional sources specifically

Both groups gave informal sources little trust. The difference showed up for institutional sources. Medical journals were the most trusted source for non-endorsers and had the widest gap between groups.

Statistical details
  • Linear mixed model on trust (0–3), random intercepts for participant, claim as covariate.
  • Endorser × source type (informal vs. institutional) interaction: b = −0.73, SE = 0.04, p < .001
  • Endorser gap within informal sources: b = −0.07, p = .051. Within institutional sources: b = −0.50, p < .001
  • Mean institutional trust, both claims pooled: 1.51 (non-endorsers) vs. 0.64 (endorsers)
For communication & content strategy

A segment that rejects a claim isn't necessarily rejecting evidence. Segment audiences by which sources they trust as well as by what they believe, and test messengers for each segment instead of assuming the most authoritative source will be the most persuasive.

2 Belief change

For non-endorsers, belief change followed trust. For endorsers, it didn't.

Non-endorsers showed about twice as much belief change after institutional sources (compared to informal ones), in line with how much they trusted each. Endorsers showed the same amount of change from every source.

Statistical details
  • Linear mixed model on belief change (0–3), random intercepts for participant, claim as covariate.
  • Endorser × source type interaction: b = −0.58, SE = 0.03, p < .001
  • Endorsers showed more change than non-endorsers from informal sources (b = 0.13, p < .001) and about the same from institutional sources (b = 0.02, p = .52)
  • Trust–change correlation by source and claim: r = .09 to .43 for non-endorsers; r = −.01 to −.22 for endorsers
For message testing

With skeptical audiences, test sources directly. How much people say they trust a source didn’t tell us how much it would move them.

3 Generalized distrust

Distrust carried across institutions

The pharma claim accused drug companies, but its endorsers distrusted government statements too. They trusted a government statement about as little as a social media post (0.24 vs. 0.22). For this group, distrust seemed to extend to institutions in general.

Statistical details
  • Endorsers' mean trust in government statements: 0.18 (government claim), 0.24 (pharma claim), the lowest of the five sources under both claims, alongside social media (0.25, 0.22)
  • 180 participants endorsed both claims; 296 endorsed at least one
For choosing a messenger

Swapping one institution for another may not help with a skeptical audience. Messengers from outside institutions, or institutional evidence shared through channels the audience already uses, are worth testing.

Why this matters beyond this study

People decide whom to learn from before they decide what to believe. Every curriculum, test item, or health campaign presents its claims through some source, but audiences don’t agree on which sources are trustworthy. Knowing which sources a segment trusts or discounts is a first step toward helping people update their beliefs.

Study

COVID-19 vaccine attitudes and source trust survey. 800 U.S. adults recruited on Prolific with a balanced quota of vaccine-hesitant and vaccine-supportive participants.

My role

I led this study end to end. I wrote the grant that funded it (Cornell University Social Sciences Seed Grant), designed and built the survey, ran data collection on Prolific, analyzed the data in R, and built the Tableau Public dashboards.

Where this fits

The recommendations on this page are implications drawn from survey data. They were not tested as interventions in this study, and the belief-change measure does not show direction of change.

Data use

Everything on this page is aggregated; the smallest group shown has 196 people. Geographic and demographic breakdowns are left out because crossing them with belief status would create small cells. Analysis rebuilt for this portfolio in R (tidyverse, lme4), with charts in Chart.js and inline SVG.
