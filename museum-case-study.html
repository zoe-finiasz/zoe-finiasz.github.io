Supporting learning at science museums

Across three children's museums, 325 families played at a hands-on gears exhibit. Whether the parent, the child, or both were setting the goals for play predicted how children explored and how they used their exploration to learn.

Sample
325 parent–child dyads · 3 sites
Method
Live observation + video coding
Role
Site coordination, qualitative coding, inter-rater reliability
01
1 Research Question

What can parents do at a science museum to help their child learn?

Museums want families to leave having learned something. The current study addresses what a parent should actually do in the moment — narrate, ask, demonstrate, or step back — and whether that choice has any measurable downstream effect on what a child has learned once the visit is over.

04
4 Coding: Define Constructs
Parent Facilitation
Parent–Child Interaction (PCI) Style
Each dyad's modal pattern across the session, coded into one of three styles:
  • Parent-led — parent sets goals, uses imperatives
  • Child-led — child sets goals, parent scaffolds with questions
  • Jointly-led — goals set together; parent hints or suggests
Child Behavior
Systematic Exploration
The proportion of the session spent building a gear machine and then testing whether it worked — connecting gears together, then spinning them to see the result. Coded from behavior in 5-second video intervals.
Learning Outcome
Gear Reconstruction Score
After free play, children were given a fully disassembled version of the gear toy and 5 minutes to rebuild it from memory, scored on an ordinal 0–3 scale:
  • 0 — no pieces placed correctly
  • 1 — 1–2 pieces correct
  • 2 — 3–4 pieces correct
  • 3 — all 5 gears correct
How systematic exploration was coded
Every 5-second segment of a child's play was assigned exactly one of five behavior codes, describing the dominant action in that window. A Connecting interval immediately followed by a Spinning / testing interval is what counted as systematic exploration — the child built something, then tested whether it worked.
Individual gear
Handling one gear or piece, not yet engaging another
Connecting
Joining (or attempting to join) gears into a machine
Spinning / testing
Turning a constructed gear machine and watching it work
Not interacting
Present at the exhibit, not engaging the materials
Not codable
Child's actions weren't visible on camera

Each construct was coded independently, then tested link by link.

Dyads that were jointly-led or child-led explored more and scored higher when asked to rebuild a gear sequence from memory. Parent-led dyads scored lowest on both measures.

Controlling for age

The relationships between each of the three constructs was tested with a model that accounts for each child’s age:

✓
Facilitation → behavior
Even after controlling for children's age, parent–child interaction style significantly predicted systematic exploration — jointly-led dyads showed significantly more than parent-led dyads.
Wald χ²(2) = 12.92, p = .002
~
Facilitation → outcome
Raw reconstruction scores did differ significantly by PCI group. But once children's age was controlled for, that group difference was no longer significant — older children scored reliably higher regardless of facilitation style, and age accounted for much of the raw gap.
Wald χ²(2) = 4.04, p = .13
✓
Behavior → outcome, modestly
Systematic exploration during play correlated with reconstruction scores, and remained a significant unique predictor even with age in the model.
Wald χ²(1) = 3.65, p = .05
06
6 Recommendations

How parents and museums can support learning

For Parents
Favor questions and hints over direct instruction when helping your child at a hands-on exhibit. Dyads where the child (alone or jointly with a parent) helped set the goals for play showed significantly more of the connect-then-test exploration pattern that's linked to what children could later reconstruct on their own.
For Museums
Design exhibits and staff prompts that invite shared or child-driven goal-setting rather than step-by-step demonstration. The clearest, most age-independent finding is about behavior during play, so the strongest design lever is getting children to build-then-test.

Additional implications from the monograph:

  • 01Exhibit and space design can create room for caregivers to explore alongside children, rather than only observe or direct — since parent-directed dyads showed less systematic exploration overall.
  • 02The coding scheme itself was co-developed with museum staff and was built to generalize — it has since been extended to other exhibits at these same museums.

My Role

This study is part of a multi-institutional, NSF-funded collaboration between developmental psychology labs (UC Santa Cruz, UT Austin, Brown) and three children's museums, studying how parent–child interaction style shapes children's exploration and learning at a hands-on exhibit.

  • Served as lab manager for the Providence, RI site (Brown University), coordinating family sessions at the Providence Children's Museum gears exhibit.
  • Ran on-site data collection with families and coded recorded sessions for children's exploratory behavior and parent–child interaction style, applying the study's operationalized coding scheme.
  • Contributed RI-site data to the pool of 325 dyads underlying the study's published analyses linking who directs play to how children explore and what they retain.
Study design and statistical analysis were led by the PI team — Maureen Callanan (UC Santa Cruz), Cristine Legare (UT Austin), and David Sobel (Brown) — with named co-authors across all three sites.
