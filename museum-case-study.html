Supporting Learning at Science Museums

Across three children's museums, 325 families played at a hands-on gears exhibit. Whether the parent, the child, or both directed the play predicted how systematically children explored, and what they could later reconstruct on their own.

Sample

325 parent–child dyads · 3 museums (ages 3–6)

Method

Live observation & video coding, 5-second intervals

My role

Coding schemes & reliability (κ > .90), coder training, museum-partner coordination

Headline finding

When children helped set the goals for play, they explored more systematically — and that build → test exploration predicted what they could rebuild from memory, even after accounting for age.

Question Methods Insights
01The question

How children learn from play may depend on how their parent helps them explore.

Museums want families to leave a hands-on exhibit having learned something. Exhibit designers and learning researchers need to know whether a parent's facilitation style has any measurable effect on what a child learns.

Does a parent's facilitation style predict what a child explores at the exhibit, and what they learn from that exploration?

One way to answer this question is to look at how the parent facilitated play, what the child actually did while playing, and what the child could demonstrate on a post-test task.

02Methodology & scope

325 families, coded five seconds at a time

Parent–child dyads were video recorded during free play at a gears exhibit across three children's museums, then coded for behavior and interaction style at every 5-second interval.

Parent Facilitation
Interaction style
Each interval was coded into one of three styles. The modal code was used to categorize each dyad:
  • Parent-led — parent sets goals, uses imperatives
  • Child-led — child sets goals, parent scaffolds with questions
  • Jointly-led — goals set together; parent hints or suggests
Child Behavior
Systematic exploration
Each interval was coded for a particular behavior. One sequence of coded intervals represented systematic exploration: the proportion of the session spent building a gear machine and then testing whether it worked.
Learning Outcome
Gear reconstruction score
After free play, children had 5 minutes to rebuild a disassembled version of the toy from memory:
  • 0 — no pieces correct
  • 1 — 1–2 pieces correct
  • 2 — 3–4 pieces correct
  • 3 — all 5 correct

03Insights & recommendations

Implications for understanding learning at science museums

Two patterns in the coded data point to recommendations for parents and for museum exhibit design.

1 Facilitation style

Letting kids lead produced more systematic exploration during free play

Dyads where parent and child set goals together (jointly-led) showed reliably more of the build → test exploration pattern than parent-led dyads; child-led dyads trended the same way. Both groups also had higher raw scores on the reconstruction task. Explore how each style compares below.

Statistical details

  • Reconstruction score (raw), child-led vs. parent-led: p = .004; jointly-led vs. parent-led: p = .07

Recommendation — for parents

Favor questions and hints over direct instruction at a hands-on exhibit. Letting a child help set the goals for play is linked to more of the build → test exploration that predicted what they could later do on their own.

2 What held up, controlling for age

The exploration behavior mattered more than which style produced it

Raw reconstruction scores did differ by facilitation style, but once children's age was controlled for, that group difference was no longer significant. Older children scored higher regardless of parent facilitation style. However, even with age in the model, systematic exploration during play was linked to high scores on the learning task.

Gear reconstruction score by facilitation style (raw group means, 0–3 scale)

Raw means shown. Once age is accounted for, the differences between facilitation styles are no longer reliable — but exploration still predicts scores.

Statistical details
  • Facilitation style → exploration: Wald χ²(2) = 12.92, p = .002
  • Facilitation style → reconstruction score: significant on raw scores; Wald χ²(2) = 4.04, p = .13 with age controlled
  • Exploration → reconstruction score, age controlled: Wald χ²(1) = 3.65, p = .05

Recommendation — for museums

Design exhibits and staff prompts around facilitating systematic exploration behavior. The build → test exploration predicted what children retained, providing a more reliable design target than facilitation style or child age alone.

Why this matters beyond the museum

Any experience meant to teach — product onboarding, tutorials, learning apps — faces the same choice between step-by-step direction and guided self-exploration. The lesson from this study is to identify the behavior that drives the outcome (here, build → test), then measure and design for that behavior directly rather than for the delivery style alone.

My role

Rhode Island site (n = 112 of 325 families): helped develop the qualitative and quantitative coding schemes with museum staff, trained undergraduate coders to inter-rater agreement of κ > .90, coordinated with museum partners, and supported recruitment and IRB work. Study design and statistical analysis were led by the PI team, with named co-authors across all three sites.

Where this fits

This case study extrapolates from a peer-reviewed monograph to make recommendations for exhibit design and parent guidance. The coding scheme developed for this project was co-designed with museum staff.
