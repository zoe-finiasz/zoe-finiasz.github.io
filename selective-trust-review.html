How Children Learn From Others

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 63 published studies (6,525 children, ages 2–8) on how children decide whom to trust when learning a new word.

Sample

63 studies · 666 conditions · 6,525 children (ages 2–8)

Method

Systematic review & meta-analysis

My role

Led systematic coding of the 63-study corpus (models run by first author)

Headline finding

Most superficial methodological choices (like presentation format) didn't affect performance, but question type and task complexity did. This has direct implications for how we assess young children's learning.

02Methods

Coding 63 studies into one comparable dataset

Each study was coded for the traits tested, how the test question was framed, and various other methodological factors like participant age, sample size, and effect direction.

Each of the 63 studies manipulated the informant's traits — epistemic (accuracy, expertise), nonepistemic (accent, attractiveness, consensus), or both at once. When a study tested both together, the two traits either favored learning from two different informants (coded below as in conflict — e.g., the more accurate informant was also the less attractive one) or from the same informant (coded as no conflict). 03Insights

Implications for assessing learning in young children

Three patterns in the coded corpus point to practical relevance for measurement and learning-material design.

1 Task complexity

Simplicity improves performance

Using the epistemic-only studies as a reference point, a clear pattern emerges: pairing a consistent nonepistemic trait with epistemic information boosts performance above that baseline, while a conflicting one pulls performance below it, for every question type.

Epistemic only (reference)
Traits in conflict
Traits consistent

The conflict/consistent categories draw on fewer conditions (5–30) than the epistemic-only reference (100–228); treat the pattern as directional, not definitive.

The same complexity shows up across age. On the Endorse question, the youngest children (2–3 years) performed well above chance when they only had to track a single informant at a time, but were close to chance when two informants gave conflicting information. By age 4, that gap disappears and children handled both versions about equally well.

Schematic reconstruction of the paper's reported interaction, built from the mean logit scores reported for the youngest age band; the exact convergence point at 4+ isn't given a precise value in the paper.

Recommendation

Simpler, more consistent information may make it easier for children to show what they actually know. Reducing conflicting cues and competing informants could help avoid mistaking "can't manage competing options" for "can't use reliable information." This may be especially important for younger learners.

2 Behavioral vs. self-report

Behavioral measures showed competence earlier than self-report

Performance on each test question changed differently with age. Even the youngest children in the corpus Endorsed the label produced by the accurate informant, but their tendency to Ask the reliable informant only caught up years later. The lines below are regression fits for performance on each question type as a function of age; higher lines mean a higher likelihood of selecting the informant who gave accurate information.

Recommendation

Ask, Endorse, and Explicit Judgment seem to tap into distinct capacities with different developmental timelines, so collapsing them into a single score risks masking this difference. An instrument covering a wide age range may benefit from validating each item type separately.

Why this matters beyond this study

Children showed what they knew through their choices before they could say it — a version of the say/do gap that shows up with adult users and consumers too. What people do and what they report often diverge, so pairing behavioral measures (choices, task success, usage) with self-report gives a truer read than either alone.

3 What didn't matter

Presentation format is a safe place to cut costs

Across question types, most of the commonly-varied methodological choices didn't move performance — including how the informant was presented (photo, video, live actor, or puppet).

Recommendation

Most methodological choices did not seem to affect performance. For instance, presentation format (video versus live) did not appear to change how well children tracked the more reliable informant. For a scalable or remote instrument, decisions may favor cost and logistics over presentation format.

My role

I led the systematic coding of the study corpus into a structured dataset. The statistical meta-analysis itself (including the models behind the findings in Sections 02–03) was run by the first author.

Where this fits

This case study builds on the existing publication to give other researchers and instrument designers in this space a clearer read on which methodological choices matter — and which don't — before they build the next study or assessment.
