Research case study

A word can do the work of a hundred data points

When someone tells you "blickets make the machine go," you generalize further than when they say "this blicket makes the machine go" — even looking at identical evidence. Two studies, 1,371 participants ages 4 to 86, test how far instructional phrasing pulls people away from what they actually observed.

The instructional problem

Curriculum and assessment language makes this choice constantly

Textbooks, science curricula, and test items routinely describe a category's properties in one of two grammatical forms — and the choice isn't neutral. Compare:

Generic claim "Sharks attack swimmers."
Specific / quantified claim "This shark attacked one swimmer." / "10% of sharks have attacked a swimmer."

The generic form implies the property is central and widespread — regardless of how rare it actually is. The specific and quantified forms tie the claim to a scope a learner can verify. If a learner then observes real, probabilistic evidence — a textbook figure, a lab result, a sample of cases — which phrasing they heard first changes how far they generalize beyond what they saw. That's the mechanism this project tests directly.

The design

Two studies, one manipulation, two audiences

Both studies used the same core setup: participants heard one of four types of testimony about a novel category (a "blicket"), then watched a sample of objects pass through a machine, some proportion of which had a causal property. Afterward, they estimated how many new objects from the category would have the property.

The one deliberate difference: adults saw evidence ranging from 10% to 100% prevalence, letting us trace a full dose-response curve. Children all saw a fixed, low evidence rate (30%) — the condition most likely to create real conflict between what they were told and what they saw, which is the sharpest test of whether testimony overrides observation.

What adults did

Generic language flattens sensitivity to evidence

Adults' estimates tracked the evidence closely under Label Only and Specific phrasing — their estimates rose almost exactly in step with the true rate. Generic phrasing broke that relationship: it pulled estimates up when evidence was low and down when evidence was high, flattening the slope significantly (interaction b = −0.166, p = .001). "All" claims shifted every estimate upward by a roughly constant amount without changing how closely people tracked the evidence — a different mechanism producing a similar-looking overestimate.

What children did

The same pull shows up before age six — more strongly

All children saw identical evidence: 3 of 10 objects had the property. Every condition overestimated it, but not equally. Specific phrasing was the only testimony that pulled children's estimates back toward what they'd actually seen; Generic and "All" phrasing pushed them further away.

Does it hold across development?

Same ordering, bigger swing for children

Restricting adults to the matching 30%-evidence condition and putting both groups on the same 0–10 scale, the ordering across conditions is consistent at both ages: Specific lowest, then Label Only and Generic, then All highest. Children's estimates are shifted upward and spread further from the truth at every condition — consistent with prior work showing that children generally defer to testimony more than adults do, on top of the language effect itself.

Instructional implication

Precision in phrasing is a design lever, not a style choice

If a curriculum or assessment item states a fact generically ("Mammals give live birth") rather than with an explicit scope or quantifier ("Most mammals give live birth" or "This mammal gives live birth"), learners — from age four through adulthood — will generalize further beyond the stated evidence than the material intends, and will be less responsive to corrective evidence presented afterward. For instructional designers, this argues for treating generic phrasing as a deliberate choice reserved for claims that are genuinely meant to generalize broadly — and for using specific or quantified language when the goal is precision, especially in materials aimed at younger learners, who show this effect most strongly.
