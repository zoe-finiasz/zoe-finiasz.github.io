Instructional language research

Instructional Language and Learning

Tell someone "sharks attack swimmers" and they'll generalize further than if you say "this shark attacked a swimmer." Two studies test how far differences in phrasing pull people's beliefs away from what they actually observed.

Sample
1,371 learners
1,249 adults (MTurk), 122 children (ages 4–6)
Design
Randomized controlled trial (between-subjects)
Analysis
R (tidyverse); dose-response modeling
Headline finding

Generic phrasing flattens people's sensitivity to the evidence they observe with their own eyes, and children ages 4–6 are affected even more than adults.

Challenge Methodology & Scope Insights
01Challenge

Minor changes in phrasing can lead to major impacts on what people learn

Textbooks, curricula, and test items need to make intentional choices about how to phrase claims about a category.

Generic claim "Sharks attack swimmers."
Specific claim "This shark attacked a swimmer."
Quantified claim "10% of sharks have attacked a swimmer."

Generic phrasing implies a property is universal; specific phrasing ties it to evidence people can check. That difference shapes how far people generalize once they see real data for themselves.

02Methodology & scope

How phrasing interacts with observed evidence, tested with children and adults

Both studies used the same setup: learners heard one of four message framings about a novel category (a "blicket"), then watched a sample of objects pass through a machine, some share of which had a causal property (making the machine light up). They then estimated how many new objects from the category would share the causal property.

03Insights & recommendations

What the data show, and what to do about it

1 Adult findings

Generic language flattens sensitivity to evidence

Adults' estimates tracked the evidence closely under Label Only and Specific phrasing. Generic phrasing broke that pattern, pulling estimates up when evidence was low and down when evidence was high. "All" claims shifted every estimate upward by a similar amount, no matter the true rate.

For instructional designers

Reserve generic phrasing for claims genuinely meant to generalize broadly; use specific or quantified language when precision matters.

2 Child findings

The same effect shows up in children, only stronger

All children saw identical evidence: 3 of 10 objects activated the machine. Every message framing led them to overestimate, but not equally; specific phrasing pulled children's estimates back toward what they'd actually seen; generic and "all" phrasing pushed them further away.

For materials aimed at young learners

Avoid generic phrasing for claims that aren't universally true. Specific language was the only framing that kept children's estimates anchored to what they actually saw.

3 Developmental comparison

Children drift further from the evidence than adults, at every framing

Comparing adults in the matching 30%-evidence condition to children on the same 0–10 scale, the ordering holds at both ages: specific phrasing lowest, then label-only and generic, then all highest. But children's estimates sit higher and drift further from the truth at every framing than adults' do.

Precision in phrasing is a design lever, not a style choice

Generic phrasing ("Mammals give live birth") makes learners generalize, while an explicit scope or quantifier ("Most mammals give live birth" or "This mammal gives live birth") keeps them anchored to the evidence in front of them. Reserve generic phrasing for claims meant to generalize broadly; use specific or quantified language when precision matters.

Why this matters beyond this study

Any material that states a fact to learners (curriculum, an assessment item, app feedback) has to make a decision about phrasing. That choice shapes how accurately learners generalize from what they observe.

Published as

My role

I led the design and analysis of the developmental (child) study. For the adult study, I led the analysis and write-up, building on a design developed collaboratively with my co-authors. All analysis, modeling, and visualization on this page were rebuilt from scratch for this portfolio.

Where this fits

This case study extrapolates the published finding — a mechanism of category-based induction — into a concrete recommendation for instructional and assessment design: that the grammatical form of a claim is itself a lever over how far learners generalize beyond stated evidence.
